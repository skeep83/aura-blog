## Why textbooks fail and how to talk to strangers in English anyway

Classroom English optimizes for accuracy: correct forms, predictable questions, a teacher who already knows what you will say. Real conversation optimizes for speed: interruptions, accents, idioms, silences you have to fill yourself. Nobody in a classroom says "sorry, what?" three times — and that is exactly the repetition you need.

Strangers are better practice than friends for one specific reason: they have no patience debt to you. A friend who knows your level simplifies their English; a stranger does not, because they are also just there to talk. You get normal-speed English from a normal person, and if the conversation fails, Next resets the table.

The usual objections are access and cost. Tutors cost money. Language exchanges require scheduling, reciprocity and tolerance for partners who mostly want to practice their own target language. Random video chat removes all three problems — provided the tool filters by language.

## A practice routine that works

Ten random minutes beats one planned hour you never schedule. A simple loop across your first three sessions:

- Session 1: talk to three strangers, do not correct yourself, just keep talking.
- Session 2: ask each partner one real question about their country or work. Questions are easier than open talk.
- Session 3: play one mini-game with a match — you will hear yourself speak while distracted, which is closest to real conditions.

You will hit people who end the call in two seconds. That is not failure, it is the mechanism: Next is free, so the cost of a bad match is one tap. Track one number across the week — total minutes spoken — and you will see it climb without a single planned lesson or purchased textbook.
