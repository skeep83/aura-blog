# Dating App Fatigue: Why Random Video Chat Is the Low-Pressure Way Out

Dating app fatigue has a measurable shape: you open the app, swipe for twenty minutes, match with three people, and one of them replies "hey." The effort is in the catalog, not the conversation. I hit that wall hard this year, deleted two apps, and ended up testing the thing swiping was supposedly replacing decades ago — random 1-on-1 video chat. Specifically Aura (https://t.me/you_aura_bot?start=src_seo), which runs as a Telegram mini app. This is not a pitch that video chat finds you a spouse. It is an argument that dating app fatigue is a design problem, and random video chat is a structurally different design.

## Why Swiping Feels Like Admin Work

The mechanics explain the burnout. Swiping is a **cataloging task dressed as a social one**:

- **Decisions without feedback.** Thousands of micro-choices, almost zero signal about why. Your brain reads that as admin work, because it is.
- **The match is the finish line, so nothing starts.** Apps optimize for the match event — the dopamine hit — and hand you the actual conversation as homework. Response rates on major apps sit in the single-digit-to-teens percentage range per match for most users.
- **Profiles are auditions.** You spend effort on photos and bio copy that get judged in half a second, which is exhausting in a way that talking is not.
- **Intent is heavy.** Everyone on a dating app is evaluating you as a candidate. That is fine occasionally and corrosive daily.

Omegle understood the opposite framing and ran with it for years before shutting down in November 2023. Its successors — Chatroulette, OmeTV, Bazo, Chamet — kept the format, and some kept the moderation problems. The idea itself (talk first, judge in real time) was never the issue; policing millions of anonymous calls was.

For dating app fatigue specifically, that is usually the correct trade, because the problem was never "not enough options."
